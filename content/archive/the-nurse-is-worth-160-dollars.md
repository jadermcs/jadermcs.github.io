Take a nurse. Weigh her out, break her down into elements, and price the result. Oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, a bit of calcium and phosphorus, trace iron. [Estimates vary wildly](https://www.sciencefocus.com/the-human-body/how-much-money-is-a-human-body) depending on how you count, from a famous $1-ish figure that circulated for decades to about $160 at commodity prices, up to roughly $150,000 if you insist on pharmaceutical-grade purity. Call it $160. That is her material value. Everything she is made of, at market.

Now ask a different question: what is she worth over the next ten years?

At the US median registered-nurse wage of [$97,550](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/registered-nurses.htm), ten years of work discounted at 5% is about $750,000 in present value. And that number is a *floor* on her contribution, not a ceiling, because wages capture only the slice of value she can bargain for. The patients who don't die, the families who don't grieve, the errors caught at 3am that never become incidents: none of that shows up in her paycheck. If you price avoided deaths the way regulators do, using a [value of a statistical life](https://www.epa.gov/environmental-economics/mortality-risk-valuation) in the $7-10 million range, one prevented death in a decade dwarfs the entire salary line.

So which number is the nurse worth? $160, or $750,000, or something with more zeros?

Everyone answers instantly, and everyone answers the same way. Nobody defends the $160. The material composition is obviously, insultingly, the wrong frame.

And yet the $160 frame is exactly how a lot of public argument about fortunes works.

## Stock, flow, and the thing in between

There are two different things people mean by "value," and mixing them up causes most of the confusion.

The first is **what something is made of** — the liquidation value, the scrap value, the pile of stuff you'd be left holding if you took it apart today. The nurse's $160.

The second is **what something is expected to produce** — the discounted stream of future output, adjusted for how likely that output is to actually arrive. The nurse's $750,000.

Finance has a formal name for the second one: discounted cash flow. You project what an asset will generate, you discount future amounts because a dollar in year nine is worth less than a dollar today, and you sum. Aswath Damodaran's [lecture notes](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/eqnotes/dcfallOld.pdf) are the canonical treatment if you want the machinery. The important structural fact is that a DCF valuation is not a description of a hoard. It is a **forecast with a price attached**.

This is not a metaphor when applied to people. It's a literal, decades-old research program. Gary Becker's [*Human Capital*](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1496221) (1964) is built on exactly this: a person's economic value is the discounted present value of their future earnings stream, and education is an *investment* precisely because you pay costs now for a larger stream later. Government agencies do the arithmetic in practice — there are [published tables](https://escholarship.org/content/qt82d0550k/qt82d0550k.pdf?t=li5h7t) of present value of lifetime earnings by age and sex, which is how courts put a number on a wrongful death.

So half the analogy is already standard economics. The nurse's "potential value" is her human capital, and it's roughly four thousand times her scrap value.

## Where the risk lives

Here's the part that makes the analogy actually interesting, and it's the part that usually gets dropped.

The $750,000 is not a fact about the nurse. It's a fact about a *distribution* over futures involving the nurse, collapsed into a single number. She might burn out at year three. She might get injured. The hospital system might restructure. She might become a nurse practitioner and double the stream. She might, as [Ibbotson and colleagues put it](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/251852872_Lifetime_Financial_Advice_Human_Capital_Asset_Allocation_and_Insurance) in the personal-finance literature that treats human capital as an asset class, carry a "unique mortality risk" — the loss of the entire income stream at once.

That framing is genuinely useful: human capital as an asset with a risk profile. Some people's is bond-like (a tenured professor's stream looks like an inflation-linked bond). Some people's is stock-like and volatile (a founder, a commissioned salesperson, an athlete). And it is deeply **illiquid** — you can't sell a claim on your own future labor, and the fact that you can't is a legal choice rather than an economic necessity, which is a fun thing to sit with for a moment.

The discount rate is where risk enters the number. Riskier streams get discounted harder. A racing driver's earnings and a staff nurse's earnings, identical on paper, are not worth the same present value, because the probability of the stream surviving to year ten is different.

And there's a deeper problem underneath risk, which Frank Knight named in [*Risk, Uncertainty, and Profit*](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1496192) (1921): risk is when you can put probabilities on outcomes, uncertainty is when you can't. Most of what determines whether a valuation is right is Knightian uncertainty, not risk. Nobody had a probability distribution over "smartphones" in 2005. Knight's point was that profit itself is the *residual* left over after all the contractually fixed payments are made — it's the compensation for bearing exactly that unmeasurable uncertainty. Which means an entrepreneurial fortune is, structurally, the accumulated payoff of a bet that could have gone to zero, priced by people who also don't know how it ends.

## The fortune-as-a-hoard mistake

Now put the two halves together.

When someone's net worth is reported as $80 billion, that number is almost never a pile of anything. It's overwhelmingly equity: shares, priced by the market, and the market price is itself a discounted forecast of future profits. The [Wikipedia entry on paper wealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_wealth) is dry about it, but the substance is right — the number is what the assets *could* sell for, under current expectations, at current prices, assuming the sale doesn't move the price. Which for a founder's controlling stake it emphatically would.

So the $80 billion is the same *kind* of number as the nurse's $750,000. It's a forecast of a stream, discounted, with a risk adjustment baked into a price. It is not $80 billion of stuff sitting somewhere.

Treating it as a hoard produces a specific error: the assumption that the quantity is fixed and merely *located* in the wrong hands, so that moving it changes who has it but not how much there is. This is the [fixed pie fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_pie_fallacy) with a valuation twist. The plain fixed-pie mistake is thinking total wealth is constant. The valuation version is subtler and more common among people who'd never fall for the plain one: it's thinking that *a particular measured fortune* is a constant that survives being moved.

It often doesn't, because the measurement was conditional on the arrangement that generated it. A large fraction of what makes a founder's stake worth what it's worth is the market's forecast about that specific firm continuing to do that specific thing. Change the arrangement enough and you haven't transferred the number, you've partly evaporated it — the same way you can't harvest the nurse's $750,000 by liquidating the nurse. What you'd get is $160.

## The honest counterarguments

I want to be fair to the other side here, because the analogy can be pushed into a claim it doesn't support.

**Earnings are not the same as social contribution.** The whole DCF frame prices the *stream captured*, not the *value created*. Those come apart badly under market power, regulatory capture, rent extraction, and lucky positioning. The nurse almost certainly creates far more value than she captures; some fortunes almost certainly capture far more than they create. "Fortune = measured potential to create value" is only true where prices track social value, and that's an assumption, not a theorem. Note this cuts in a specific direction: it argues the nurse is *underpriced*, not that the framing is wrong.

**"It's only paper wealth" proves less than it seems.** Illiquid assets still purchase real things: collateral for loans, control over how capital gets deployed, political influence. And the wealth-tax literature has thought hard about the valuation objection. Saez and Zucman's [BPEA paper](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/saez-zucmanBPEAoct19.pdf) and the [critique](https://www.columbia.edu/~wk2110/bin/BPEASaezZucman.pdf) alongside it are the honest version of this debate; their [capital-gains withholding proposal](https://gabriel-zucman.eu/files/SYZ2021.pdf) is partly an attempt to route around exactly the "you can't value it" problem. You can accept every word about stocks versus flows and still think the flows should be taxed differently.

**Forecasts can be wrong in both directions.** If a valuation is a forecast, it can be inflated as easily as it can be sound. Sometimes the market's discounted stream is a bubble, and the honest description of "this fortune is a measurement of expected future production" is "this fortune is a measurement of what people currently believe about future production."

## Has anyone written this before?

Sort of, and this is what surprised me. Every individual piece is well established:

- Human capital as discounted future earnings: Becker, 1964, foundational.
- Human capital as a risky, illiquid, non-diversifiable asset class: standard in the [lifecycle finance literature](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/251852872_Lifetime_Financial_Advice_Human_Capital_Asset_Allocation_and_Insurance).
- Statistical valuation of a life for policy: [EPA and OMB](https://www.epa.gov/environmental-economics/mortality-risk-valuation), routine.
- "The body's chemicals are worth $160": [a stock science-writing trope](https://www.chemistryworld.com/opinion/how-much-are-you-worth/3005591.article), usually deployed as a humbling curiosity.
- Net worth as forecast rather than hoard: [well covered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_wealth) in finance explainers.
- Fixed-pie thinking as an error: [a whole genre](https://catalyst.independent.org/2019/12/26/there-is-no-pie-the-fallacy-of-zero-sum-economic-thinking/).

What I couldn't find is the assembly. The body-chemicals figure lives in science trivia and never gets used as an *economic* argument. The human capital literature is technical and never reaches for the scrap-value comparison to make the stock/flow distinction vivid. And the fixed-pie essays argue against the plain version of the fallacy, not the valuation version. The pieces sit in four separate literatures that don't cite each other.

Which is the point of the analogy, if it has one. Nobody needs to be argued out of pricing a nurse at $160 — the absurdity does the work instantly. The claim is that the same move, applied to a fortune, is the same category error, and it doesn't feel absurd only because we're less practiced at seeing it. The reason a nurse is worth more than her calcium is the same reason a fortune is not a stack of gold in a room: both numbers are claims on futures that might not arrive, priced under uncertainty, by people who don't know how it ends.
